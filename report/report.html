D1 Educational problem

Class 9 learners at NGO after-school centres usually meet the pendulum as a formula to memorise (T = 2π√(L/g)) and never test it themselves. Many believe that a heavier bob or a bigger push changes how long one swing takes, and few have practised a fair test that changes only one thing at a time. Each child gets the centre's shared tablet for a short turn, so learning has to happen in short sessions that can be paused and picked up later. Jhoola Lab ("jhoola" means swing) lets a learner predict, run timed swing experiments by changing length, mass and angle, and revise her idea from her own data, all within one 10-minute session.

Measurable learning outcomes

  1. Predict whether changing the length, the mass of the bob or the starting angle changes the period of a simple pendulum, and check the prediction with an experiment.
  2. Design a fair test that changes only one variable, and explain why the other two must stay the same.
  3. Explain from her own data why a longer string gives a longer period, and calculate that a string four times shorter halves the period (100 cm: 2.0 s, 25 cm: 1.0 s).

Context of use

Where: an NGO after-school centre ("Prerna") in an urban settlement in Mumbai. When: 5–7 pm, one 10-minute turn per child on a shared tablet, two or three evenings a week. Device: a shared 10-inch Android tablet with Chrome on the centre's Wi-Fi; a facilitator is in the room.

D3 Educational content

Concept explanation (Start screen, conversational, key terms highlighted)

"Hi Sana! Let's find out what decides how long one swing takes. First, the period is the time for one full swing, there and back (ek poore jhoole ka samay). Second, timing one swing is hard because our hand reacts late, so we time 10 swings and divide by 10. Third, a fair test changes only one thing at a time: the length, the mass or the angle. We keep the other two the same." A glossary gives each term with its Hindi gloss (bob = latakta hua vazan, angle = kitna kheencha, fair test = sahi tulna).

How constraint C4 (10-minute sessions) is visible

  • A countdown in the header ("06:48 left") with a progress bar, a five-step tracker, and a per-step time budget from the 10-minute plan ("Experiment: 01:20 of 5 min left").
  • Move-on nudge: when a step's budget is used, a banner says "You planned 5 min for Experiment. Move on to Check so you finish in 10 minutes", with "Go to Check" and "1 more minute". In Predict it says "First ideas are fine".
  • The clock stops at Wrap-up ("Done in 07:03"), because the learning sequence is complete there.
  • "Stop & save" on every step; every action is saved immediately under the learner's name on the shared tablet.
  • Picking your name resumes at the saved step with your trials, predictions and answers ("Welcome back, Sana! You are continuing from Experiment with 3 saved trials").
  • At 10:00 a banner offers "Stop & save" or "Finish this step first". The 4× speed option keeps 10 timed swings to about 4 seconds.

D5 Two contrasting learner scenarios (run on the live app)

(a) Ravi responds correctly

Ravi predicts "the same time" for mass and angle and "more time" for a longer string. The lab confirms each prediction ("This matches your prediction. Well done, scientist!"). He runs four trials, each a fair test: 50 cm, 100 g, 10° (1.42 s) → 300 g (1.43 s) → 20° (1.41 s) → 25 cm (1.00 s). He answers 1.0 s, picks pair C and writes an evidence-based explanation, which the coach marks correct. The summary ticks all three outcomes.

(b) Sana shows misconception M1 ("heavier swings faster")

Sana predicts that a heavier stone takes less time and that a bigger pull takes more time. The lab starts her with the mass experiment, because that is where her prediction disagrees with physics. Her second trial changes the mass and the length (an unsuccessful experiment). The app names both changes, says the cause cannot be known, and offers a hint ladder without revealing which variable matters. With the fair-test helper she changes only the mass back to 100 g: the period stays about the same (1.80 s → 1.79 s), and the app gives the M1 feedback and asks "What do you think now?". In Check she answers 0.5 s (the linear-scaling gap) and writes "The heavier stone swings faster…". The coach flags M1 and sends her back to her mass trials.

How the system responds differently: the experiment order (mass first for Sana), the result card (confirmation vs misconception feedback plus revise buttons), the scaffolding (none vs the hint ladder and helper after an unfair test), the numeric feedback (praise vs the linear-scaling explanation with a lab link) and the coach reply (evidence praise vs a Socratic prompt back to her data).

System prompt (exact)

You are "Lab coach" in Jhoola Lab, a virtual pendulum lab for a Class 9 student at an NGO after-school centre in Mumbai. She speaks Hindi at home and reads simple English.
You receive her written answer to one question, her experiment trials and her earlier predictions.
Decide which idea her answer shows and write one short coaching reply.
Labels:
- "correct": she says the mass of the bob does not change the period, ideally using numbers from her trials.
- "M1_mass": she thinks a heavier bob swings faster or slower (mass changes the period).
- "M2_angle": she thinks a bigger angle or push changes the time for one swing.
- "unclear": anything else.
Rules for "feedback":
- Simple English, at most 2 short sentences and 45 words. Friendly; use "we" or "let's".
- Point her to her own trials, for example a fair test where only the mass changed.
- If the label is not "correct", never state the answer (do not say that mass or angle does not change the period). Ask a question that makes her check her data.
- If the label is "correct", praise her use of evidence in one sentence.
Reply with JSON only, no other text: {"label": "...", "feedback": "..."}

Design judgement: the simulation itself uses no AI. Periods come from T = 2π√(L/g) with a small stopwatch error, so the data a learner reasons from is always physically correct. AI is used only where language understanding helps (free-text explanations), and it is checked and replaceable.

D7 Pedagogical rationale

  • Inquiry cycle (predict, observe, explain): predictions are made first and treated as hypotheses ("there is no wrong answer here"). The lab then confronts each one with the learner's own data, and she is asked to revise her idea ("My data changed my idea" / "I want to test again"). Conceptual change comes from her evidence, not from being told.
  • Control of variables: the fair-test idea is taught up front ("change only one thing"), the table highlights changed cells in yellow after every trial, and the lab only counts a test when exactly one variable changed.
  • Feedback and scaffolding: immediate, specific feedback on every task. Misconception feedback names the common belief kindly ("many of us think…") to protect a first-generation learner's confidence. Scaffolds fade: hint 1 asks a question, hint 2 points at the table rows, hint 3 offers the fair-test helper.
  • Design challenge (learning from an unsuccessful experiment without revealing the answer): when two variables change, the app says what changed and why no conclusion is possible, never which variable matters. The hint ladder rebuilds the method (choose one variable, compare rows, use the helper). The physics is explained only after the learner's own fair test has produced the evidence (screens b2–b5).
  • Adaptivity: profile: predictions are a diagnostic and decide the experiment order (Sana tests mass first); performance: a wrong numeric answer or a misconception in the explanation triggers targeted feedback and a link back to the lab; interaction: a second unfair test jumps straight to the fair-test helper, and hint use is logged.
  • Constraint C4 and the learner context: shared tablets mean short, interrupted turns, so the whole activity is budgeted to 10 minutes with a visible clock, step budgets and a time-up prompt. A name-based profile picker gives per-child stop and resume on one device. 4× speed keeps each trial to about 4 seconds, and the activity is one screen per step with large touch targets.
  • Language and engagement: short, conversational sentences ("Let's find out…", "we"), verbal cues (First, Second, Third), highlighted key terms, and Hindi glosses for every key term. Lexend is used because it was designed for readability.
  • Contrasting cases ("Race two pendulums"): two pendulums that differ in only one thing swing side by side, after a prediction. Seeing a light and a heavy bob stay in step is direct, visual evidence against M1. Seeing a 25 cm pendulum swing twice for every swing of a 100 cm one makes the √L relation visible.
  • Multiple representations for M2: the dashed swing path and the live speed bar show that a bigger angle gives a longer path and a higher speed (for example 35 cm at 0.39 m/s for 10°, 70 cm at 0.77 m/s for 20°), so the time stays the same. The 3D view (turnable camera, floor shadow) helps learners who have never seen a real lab stand picture the set-up; the 2D view keeps the measuring tools.
  • Pacing for C4: a learner who takes too long in one step is nudged on with a concrete next action rather than cut off, so a complete sequence fits in 10 minutes. The countdown shows time left, not time used, which is easier for a 14-year-old to act on. Outcomes on the summary are ticked only from evidence, and each missing tick says exactly what to redo (feedback for self-regulation).
  • Transfer to daily life: the start screen shows a swinging playground jhoola with a hook question ("a jhoola with 2 m ropes takes about 2.8 seconds for one full swing, whoever sits on it. Why?").
  • Wrap-up examples: the wrap-up links the idea to the playground jhoola, pendulum clocks and a 25 cm thread experiment at home (about 10 s for 10 swings).
  • Topic-specific tools: stopwatch (10-swing timing), ruler (string length in cm with a red marker), protractor (starting angle), data table with change highlighting, and a period-vs-length graph.

Reflection

  • What AI did well: it turned the card into a coherent design quickly, wrote working code, and tested the live app end to end, which gave me screenshots of both paths.
  • Where AI was wrong and how it was caught: the first version of the fair-test helper copied the earlier trial instead of the latest one, so the "fair" test actually tested a different variable. The browser test caught it and it was fixed and redeployed at 18:40. The first Vercel deployment failed because the framework preset was missing, which was fixed in vercel.json.
  • A gap I found by testing: typing "yes it is" in Task 7 gave a general reply, although "yes" to "does a heavier bob change the period?" is misconception M1. The rule-based coach now reads yes/no answers (including haan/nahi) and gives the M1 reply.
  • My judgement about AI in the product: I kept AI out of the simulation so learners always reason from correct data. AI only reads free-text explanations. Its output is validated, cross-checked with rules, must not give away the answer, and always has a labelled fallback.
  • What I checked myself: the physics values (T = 2π√(L/g): 50 cm gives 1.42 s, 25 cm gives 1.00 s, 100 cm gives 2.01 s), that the misconceptions and feedback fit Sana's persona, and that the identity token appears on every screen.
